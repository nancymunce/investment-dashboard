# The Veteran's Desk

*Notes from forty-plus years of watching markets — and mostly watching myself. Edit this file any time; the dashboard renders it as-is.*

## Market view

I don't know what the market will do next month, and after four decades I have stopped pretending otherwise. What I do know is that the patient owner of productive assets has always been paid eventually, and the impatient trader of predictions has almost always paid instead.

This portfolio is built on process, not prediction. Roughly two-thirds sits in broad, low-cost funds that own thousands of businesses — that is the admission ticket to long-term compounding. The remaining third is a handful of excellent companies bought with a margin of safety and held for years. Nobody here is trying to be clever quarter to quarter. Clever is expensive.

Expect long stretches where this looks boring. Boring is the point. The money is made in the years you do nothing.

## Rebalancing guidance

Prices move; targets don't. Once a year — pick a date and keep it — compare each holding's actual weight to its target. If anything has drifted more than five percentage points from target, trim it back and feed the laggards. That's it. No committee meeting required.

Between annual checkups, leave it alone. Rebalancing is maintenance, like rotating tires: necessary, unglamorous, and not something you do every morning.

One exception: if a single stock ever grows past 8% of the portfolio, or one sector past 25%, don't wait for the calendar. Trim it. Concentration is how fortunes are made and how they are lost — and this portfolio was not designed to gamble.

## Behavioral wisdom

- **The news is not your advisor.** Headlines are written to be clicked, not to be followed. If a headline makes you want to act today, that is precisely the day to do nothing.
- **Never sell in a panic; never buy in a frenzy.** Write your plan when you are calm — this page is that plan — and let the calm version of you outvote the frightened one.
- **Don't chase hot stocks.** By the time a stock is the talk of every gathering, its best years are usually behind it. The portfolio already owns quality; let it work.
- **Judge decisions by process, not outcome.** A good decision can lose money for a year. A bad decision can make money for a year. Only the process repeats.
- **Costs are the one return you control.** Every dollar not paid in fees is a dollar compounding for you instead of someone else. That is why the core of this portfolio is measured in basis points.

## What could go wrong — honestly

No honest investor sells you a portfolio without telling you how it breaks. Here is how this one breaks:

1. **A long US bear market.** Two-thirds of this portfolio leans on American equities. In a grinding multi-year decline, diversification softens the blow — it does not prevent it.
2. **Rising rates.** Bonds are supposed to be the ballast, but when rates climb, BND falls at the same time stocks wobble. The "safe" third can feel anything but.
3. **A single-stock blowup.** Eight individual stocks means eight chances to be wrong about a company. Position limits keep any one mistake small, but small is not zero.
4. **Taiwan.** TSM is a wonderful business sitting in the most geopolitically sensitive spot on the map. That risk is real, sized at 5%, and worth naming plainly.
5. **Tracking regret.** This portfolio will underperform the S&P 500 for years at a time — that is the price of diversification and defense. The danger isn't the underperformance; it's abandoning the plan because of it.
6. **You.** The most reliable way to ruin a good portfolio is to override it at the worst moment. The plan only works if you let it.

> "In the short run, the market is a voting machine; in the long run, it is a weighing machine." — Benjamin Graham. Forty years on, I have found no reason to doubt him, and many reasons I wish I had listened sooner.
